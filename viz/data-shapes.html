Onboarding · synthetic data · sketches 1–4

Odysee Air, drawn four ways

Four throwaway pictures of the synthetic airline that ships in odysee-data-warehouse. Each one takes a single relationship from the data model and draws it. Every pattern here was put there by the simulator, so read these for the shape of the data, not for facts about a real airline.

The same month of February 2027 counted at five grains. Each step to the right is a one-to-many expansion.

Sketch 1 · the plan

A schedule is 39 aircraft drawing zigzags through one day

Time runs left to right, airports are stacked west to east. Each line is one leg. The blue path is one physical aircraft working through its day: its rotation. The flat dashed parts are the aircraft sitting on the ground. Hover a line to trace its aircraft, click to keep it.

What to notice. Lines bunch at the two hub rows, PIT and GSO, in waves: many arrivals, a gap, many departures. Those waves are banks, and the gap is where connections happen. What is flat in this data: all 324 bands say "every day, 1–28 Feb", so the month strip is 28 identical rows. A real SSIM file has bands that differ by weekday and date range, and that strip would show it.

Sketch 2 · reality meets the plan

One leg, many journeys

Take one flight on one day and ask each passenger where their trip really starts and ends. Local passengers are only flying this leg. Flow passengers are using it as half of a longer trip. The aircraft sees one full cabin. The revenue system sees a dozen different markets sharing it.

What to notice. A flow passenger's fare covers two legs, so only part of it is credited to this one. The simulator splits it by miles flown. That split is called prorating, and it is why seat share and revenue share on a leg are different numbers. Simulator assumptions: about 35% of traffic is flow across the whole network (both flights shown here run far above that), connections are at most two legs, and nobody is assigned a seat or a cabin.

Sketch 3 · reality over time

A flight fills up backwards from departure

The same flight again, but now along the time it was sold. Read left to right as the departure approaches. The horizontal axis is stretched so the last two weeks, where most of the action is, get half the width.

What to notice. Leisure travellers book early and cheap, business travellers book late and pay more, and the black staircase is the pricing rule that makes that true. Real airlines call the upper picture a booking curve, and comparing a flight's curve with its history is how they judge whether it is selling ahead or behind. Simulator assumptions: the staircase is hard-coded (five price steps at 60, 21, 14 and 7 days), the booking-time distributions are fixed formulas, and nobody cancels after booking. Seats are also handed out in the order the simulator invents passengers, not in the order they booked, so a sold-out flight does not turn away late bookers the way a real one would.

Sketch 4 · the plan, as waves

Each aircraft is a wave, and a hub is what you get when the waves line up

The zigzags from sketch 1, redrawn. Each row is one aircraft and its height is how far it is from its hub. Out to a spoke is a crest, back at the hub is a trough. The schedule repeats daily, so two days are drawn. Underneath, the waves are added together.

What to notice. When the troughs line up, the sum drops and the count of aircraft on the ground at the hub spikes. That moment is a bank: many aircraft at the hub at once, so passengers can step between them. GSO does this cleanly four times a day. PIT does it hard first thing in the morning and then smears into a steadier flow. Press the second button and the same aircraft fly the same legs, just out of step. The sum goes flat and the short connections go with it. The cost of lining up: short flights have to wait on the ground for long ones, which is the flat part at the bottom of each wave. Limits: the connection counts use timing only (35 to 240 minutes between an arrival and a departure on a different aircraft to a different city). The random slide ignores night-time, so it is a thought experiment and not a schedule anyone could fly.
