Hub compass

Stand at the hub and look outward. Every other airport sits on the rim in compass order: north at the top, east on the right. Each band across the wheel is a pair of cities whose passengers changed planes here in February 2027, and its thickness is how many. A band that crosses the middle is the hub carrying people straight through, from one side of the map to the other. A band that hugs the rim is people doubling back.

Hover an airport to see who it connects to. Click to keep it.

What to notice. The wheel answers a question the earlier pictures could not: what is this hub for? Pittsburgh's thickest bands join the East Coast to the Midwest (Chicago, Detroit) and to Greensboro, the other hub. Greensboro's join the north to Florida. The grey part of each airport's arc is everyone who was only going to the hub itself, so an airport with a long grey arc and thin bands uses the hub as a destination, and one with a short grey arc uses it as a stepping stone. Switch hubs and the same airports rearrange, because compass order is measured from wherever you are standing.

Limits. Airports are in compass order but spaced by traffic, not by true angle, so the wheel is not a map: the crowded Northeast takes far more of the rim than its real slice of the horizon. The "turn" figures in the panel use true bearings. Bands are drawn in one neutral colour because with forty airports no colour scheme stays readable; hover does the work instead. The simulator only sells a connection if the route is at most 1.5 times the direct distance, which is why very few bands double back. Passenger and fare totals count each direction of travel once.
