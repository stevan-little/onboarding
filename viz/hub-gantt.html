Fleet Gantt

The chart every schedule planner already reads: one row per aircraft, time across, a bar for each flight. The layout is left alone. What changes is that each bar now carries what happened on that flight, and the strips above each hub add the rows up, so the plan and its results sit in one picture. What is added to the familiar chart

  • A bar is a container. Its outline is the seats on the aircraft, so the 64-seat regional jets draw shorter. It fills from the bottom with seats sold: blue for passengers only flying that flight, orange for passengers connecting.
  • A dark cap is demand that did not fit. It counts seat requests the flight could not take. A doubled cap means more than about 50. A full bar with no cap was just enough aircraft; a full bar with a tall cap was too little.
  • A shaded gap is an aircraft on the ground at its hub. Where shaded gaps line up down the page, many aircraft are at the hub together. That is a bank, and the grey strip above each hub is those gaps counted.
  • The orange strip is people. It counts passengers who have got off one flight at the hub and not yet left on the next. The familiar chart shows aircraft meeting; this shows how many people were in between.
  • Click a flight to see its connections. Orange ribbons run to the flights its passengers went on to, or came from, in proportion to the number of people. Thin grey lines are legal connections nobody used. Red dashes are near misses. The tinted band is the window in which a connection is allowed.
  • "Against its usual weekday" recolours each bar by how that day compares with the same flight on the month's other three days of that weekday.

What February looks like through it (whole-month figures)

    Patterns worth hunting. Order the rows by "same airports together": about half the fleet shares its airports with one other aircraft (bracketed on the left), and nearly every aircraft flies the same short loop two or more times a day (the ×2 beside its name). Compare the two halves of any row: the same route flown morning and afternoon rarely fills the same way. Step through the days with the arrow keys and the fills breathe with the week. In the strips, compare the aircraft count with the passenger count under it: where passengers stay high while aircraft thin out, people are waiting longer for their next flight. Zoom into one part of the day to read flight numbers and turn times.

    Limits. A cap counts requests, not people: a booking that tries a flight and cannot be seated is counted there, and the same traveller can be counted on several flights before finding a seat or giving up. A flight can show a cap without being full when a party was larger than the seats left. The simulator hands seats out in the order it invents passengers, not in booking order, so which market is turned away is arbitrary, though how many is not. Connections follow the simulator's rules (35 minutes minimum, 60 if any airport is outside the US, at most 4 hours, same day, not back to the origin, route at most 1.5 times direct), and unlike my earlier hub pages this one counts staying on the same aircraft as a connection, because the simulator does. Revenue is fare money booked to the flight with connecting fares split by miles. "Usual weekday" rests on three other days, so it is noisy. I have not used Sabre's planning tools; "the familiar chart" means the standard aircraft-rotation Gantt.
