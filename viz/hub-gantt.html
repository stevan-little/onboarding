Fleet Gantt

The chart every schedule planner already reads: one row per aircraft, time across, a bar for each flight. The layout is left alone. What changes is that each bar now carries what happened on that flight, the gaps between bars carry where the aircraft was waiting, and the strips above each hub add the rows up, so the plan and its results sit in one picture. What the bars, gaps and strips add

  • A bar is a container. Its outline is the seats on the aircraft, so the 64-seat regional jets draw shorter. It fills from the bottom with seats sold: blue for passengers only flying that flight, orange for passengers connecting.
  • A red cap is demand that did not fit. It counts requests for a seat that found the flight full. A full bar with no cap was just enough aircraft; a full bar with a tall cap was too little.
  • A shaded gap is an aircraft waiting at its hub. Where shaded gaps line up down the page, many aircraft are at the hub together. That is a bank, and the first strip above each hub is those gaps counted.
  • The second strip is people. It counts passengers who have got off one flight at the hub and not yet left on the next. A conventional chart shows aircraft meeting; this shows whether anyone used the meeting.
  • Click a flight to see its connections. Orange ribbons run to the flights its passengers went on to, or came from, as thick as the number of people. Thin grey lines are connections nobody used that day. Red dashes are near misses.

What February looks like through it

    Patterns worth hunting. Order the rows by "same airports together" and most aircraft turn out to have a twin flying the mirror image of their day, and nearly every aircraft flies the same short loop two or more times (the ×2 beside its name, with a small hook under the row where each loop restarts). Step through the days (the arrow keys work) and the fills breathe with the week while the caps stay on the same few rows. "Against its own average" strips the usual level away and leaves only what is unusual about the day you picked. Compare the two halves of any row: the same route flown morning and afternoon rarely fills the same way. In the strips, compare the aircraft count with the passenger count under it. Where the passengers stay high while the aircraft thin out, people are waiting longer for the next flight, which is what Pittsburgh's afternoon looks like.

    Limits. A "refused request" is a booking that tried a flight and found too few seats left; the same traveller can be refused by several flights before finding one, so caps overstate lost passengers (the count of trips that found no seat on any flight is in the findings). The simulator hands seats out in the order it invents passengers, not in booking order, so which market gets refused is arbitrary, though how many is not. Connections follow the simulator's rules (35 minutes minimum, 60 if any airport is outside the US, at most 4 hours, same day, not back to the origin, route at most 1.5 times direct). Revenue is fare money booked to the flight with connecting fares split by miles. I have not used Sabre's planning tools; "the familiar chart" here means the standard aircraft-rotation Gantt, and the comparison is with that general form.
