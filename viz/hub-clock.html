The hub as a clock

One hub's whole day wrapped around a 24-hour dial, midnight at the top. Every arrival is a blue dot on the inner band and every departure an orange dot on the outer band, placed at its clock time. Each grey thread inside the dial is one connection: a passenger could step off that arrival and onto that departure. The longer the wait, the deeper the thread sags toward the centre.

The next step from here is Hub Retimer: this clock with a dial on every flight.

What to notice. Dots come in clumps, and the clumps alternate: a clump of arrivals, then a clump of departures. Each pair is a bank. Threads that stay near the rim are the short waits a bank is built to create; threads that sag deep are passengers waiting for the next bank. Pick a flight and two shaded wedges appear: the red one is the minimum connecting time, where a departure leaves too soon to catch, and the tinted one is the window where connections are allowed. Drag the wait slider to move the far edge of that window and watch threads appear and vanish. Near misses are pairs that would connect if the second flight left a few minutes later. Ranking those is what the Connectivity product's "opportunities" are.

Rules and limits. These are the simulator's rules: minimum connecting time 35 minutes (60 if any airport involved is outside the US), no connecting back to where you came from, and a route no more than 1.5 times the direct distance. The simulator allows waits up to 4 hours; the real connectivity engine allows 6 and looks up the minimum per carrier, terminal and aircraft. A near miss here is a wait of zero up to just under the minimum. Every thread counts the same regardless of how many people would want that trip.
